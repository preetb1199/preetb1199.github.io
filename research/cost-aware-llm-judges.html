Research question

How closely can a small adaptive set of LLM judges approach a much larger optimal routing policy?

Approach

  • Profiled 18 binary LLM verifiers on 1,084 labeled mathematical reasoning tasks.
  • Modeled model-specific accuracy, cost, and stochastic latency.
  • Implemented dynamic-programming comparisons with roughly 16 million states.
  • Ran large-scale Monte Carlo experiments across error tolerances and latency penalties.

Findings & status

  • Evaluated around 82 million Monte Carlo trajectories over 170 experimental settings.
  • Adaptive two-LLM policies approached the 18-model Bellman benchmark within a few percent in studied configurations.

Comparisons are numerical and specific to the studied verifiers, model assumptions, and penalty settings.

Problem formulation & decision model

In sequential binary verification, an evaluation pipeline decides at each step whether to stop and accept/reject a candidate output or query another LLM verifier with known accuracy, token cost, and stochastic latency distribution. Formulating this trade-off as a Markov decision process yields a Bellman dynamic-programming benchmark over roughly 16 million states across 18 candidate verifiers. Comparing restricted two-model adaptive routing policies against the full 18-model Bellman optimum quantifies how much efficiency is achievable without managing a large heterogeneous model fleet.

Technical Q&A

Why can two adaptive LLM judges approximate an 18-model optimal router? ▾

A fast, inexpensive verifier resolves high-confidence items immediately while escalating ambiguous cases to a stronger verifier. Across the studied mathematical-reasoning verifiers and latency penalties, most cost–accuracy gains come from this two-tier separation rather than fine-grained switching among many intermediate models.

How does cost-aware judge routing connect to statistical LLM auditing? ▾

Cost-aware routing lowers per-item inference and latency costs during verification, while human-grounded auditing methods such as MetaAudit (PPI++) guarantee statistically valid population confidence intervals even when automated verifiers have residual error.
