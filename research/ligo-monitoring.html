Research question

How can gravitational-wave detectors be monitored continuously to verify that injected signals are recovered faithfully?

Published abstract

To ensure that faint continuous gravitational-wave (CW) signals from fast-spinning galactic neutron stars can be detected during the O4 observing run, 18 simulated CW hardware injections are added directly into Advanced LIGO data by modulating interferometer mirror positions across the LIGO detection band. Daily and weekly monitoring verifies detector response, end-to-end search pipelines, data cleaning, and preservation of absolute phase over as many as 10¹¹ signal cycles. The paper compares three monitoring estimators: a highly templated matched filter for precise amplitude and phase extraction, a frequentist F-statistic marginalizing over amplitude, phase, and orientation, and a Bayesian reconstruction of source parameters with noise characterization—with the templated estimator validating absolute timing delays in the detector response and data stream.

Approach

  • Developed a templated matched-filter estimator for injected continuous-wave signals.
  • Recovered amplitude, phase, and timing for 18 test signals spanning approximately 12–2,991 Hz.
  • Used 1,800-second Fourier segments, adaptive power windows, noise estimation, and inverse-variance weighting.
  • Operated the analysis as an automated daily pipeline on the Caltech computing cluster.

Findings & status

  • Approximately 44% lower amplitude uncertainty relative to established estimators in the reported comparison.
  • Timing measurements at the few-microsecond level (approximately 3–6 μs offsets) supported detector validation.
  • The system was run as a daily research-monitoring pipeline rather than just a standalone simulation.

Reported comparisons describe the published detector-calibration study and its specific estimators and test signals.

Estimator comparison & signal-processing context

Continuous-wave (CW) hardware injections actuate Advanced LIGO test masses directly to synthesize persistent, quasi-monochromatic signals from hypothetical spinning neutron stars across ~12–2,991 Hz. Because coherent CW searches track phase across up to 10¹¹ signal cycles, small timing drifts or calibration errors can degrade astrophysical sensitivity. The CQG 2026 study benchmarks three complementary validation estimators on 1,800-second Fourier segments: a templated matched filter (delivering the tightest amplitude and absolute phase/timing precision), a frequentist ℱ-statistic (marginalizing over unknown amplitude, phase, and orientation), and a Bayesian parameter-reconstruction pipeline with explicit noise characterization. Related open-source Python tooling for grouped semi-coherent CW SNR and template-spacing analysis is available in CalculateSNRg_Python ↗.

Technical Q&A

Why are continuous-wave hardware injections injected physically into LIGO mirrors? ▾

Modulating the interferometer mirrors via the calibration actuation system exercises the full end-to-end physical sensing chain, calibration transfer function, data-conditioning pipelines, and downstream search software—catching timing or phase discrepancies that software-only injections cannot detect.

How does timing accuracy relate to continuous-wave phase preservation? ▾

At kilohertz signal frequencies, microsecond-level timing offsets map directly to measurable phase shifts. Recovering absolute phase across 18 injected signals spanning 12–2,991 Hz validates detector timing offsets down to the ~3–6 μs scale.
