When Confidence Breaks Silently: Calibration, Discrimination & OOD Failure Detection

Why low Expected Calibration Error can mask total loss of ranking signal—and why naive failure-detector gating can backfire

Evaluating an AI decision agent solely by accuracy or pooled Expected Calibration Error (ECE) obscures how decisions fail when real-world inputs degrade. Drawing on 847,838 recorded API calls across seven corruption families in CORA, this guide explains the separation between calibration and discrimination, out-of-distribution detector failure, and architectural safeguards.

EMPIRICAL ANCHOR

Primary DeepSeek V4 Flash held-out controlled track (n = 9,000): discrimination fell steeply while pooled ECE stayed near zero.

1. Mathematical separation: Calibration (ECE) vs. Discrimination (AUROC)

Murphy's classic decomposition of the Brier score partitions probabilistic prediction error into three orthogonal terms: Brier = Reliability − Resolution + Uncertainty. Expected Calibration Error (ECE) tracks Reliability—whether predictions labeled "60%" occur 60% of the time in aggregate. AUROC tracks Resolution / Discrimination—the probability that a randomly chosen positive case receives a higher score than a randomly chosen negative case.

A trivial base-rate predictor returning 0.50 on every balanced case achieves ECE = 0.000 while delivering zero decision value (AUROC = 0.500). Under silent staleness—twenty-day-old evidence whose target five-day window has already elapsed—DeepSeek V4 Flash fell from 0.661 clean AUROC straight to chance (0.500), while pooled held-out ΔECE stayed 0.000 [−0.016, 0.016] due to opposing per-family calibration drifts canceling in the pooled average.

2. Testing transfer, not memorization: 3 seen vs. 4 held-out corruptions

To prevent monitors from memorizing corruption templates, CORA separates development corruptions (numeric noise, evidence dropout, labelled staleness) from four unseen test corruptions (silent staleness, contradiction, sentiment reversal, prompt injection) across controlled equities, a freight-delivery transfer domain, and a real-news sanity check.

Across the independent freight-transfer track, all four model configurations similarly exhibited held-out AUROC drops of approximately 0.098–0.113 alongside positive calibration drift. Furthermore, direct verbal JSON probabilities outperformed token-derived yes/no probabilities for primary DeepSeek (clean ECE 0.043 vs. 0.158; AUROC 0.661 vs. 0.609), and extra reasoning effort did not rescue discrimination under corrupted evidence.

3. Why out-of-distribution failure detectors fail—and hurt downstream decisions

A gradient-boosted tree detector trained on the three seen corruptions collapsed to 0.428 AUROC (below chance) on the held-out families for primary DeepSeek, compared with 0.666 for confidence-only and 0.702 for agent self-reported warning flags—and even self-reported flags dropped to chance (0.503) on silent staleness alone.

Crucially, evaluating the monitor inside a simulated downstream long–short policy (30% held-out corruption prevalence, 200 assignments) showed that detector gating reduced annualized return by −3.35 percentage points [−6.08, −1.25] relative to naive execution. An uncalibrated OOD detector discards valid opportunities faster than it filters corrupted inputs.

4. Practical architectural takeaway: validate freshness at ingestion

A behavioral detector observing an agent's output cannot reliably infer the true age of an unlabelled stale input from prose alone. Reliable agentic pipelines must enforce ingestion-time timestamp validation, source-integrity verification, and schema lineage checks prior to model inference—and evaluate every proposed mitigation on actual downstream decision outcomes rather than detector AUROC alone.
